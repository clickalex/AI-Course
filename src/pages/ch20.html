Recommenders, search & knowledge graphs

Most of the internet’s ML is ranking: what to show next, which document answers the query, which entity is this.

Figure 20.1 — Ranking over a graph of users, items and entities.

  • Collaborative filtering — users who liked X liked Y (matrix factorisation, implicit ALS).
  • Content-based — item features.
  • Two-tower / two-stage rankers — retrieve then re-rank (the industrial pattern, also RAG).
  • IR — BM25 lexical + dense embeddings + hybrid + cross-encoder rerank.
  • Knowledge graphs — (head, relation, tail) triples; entity linking; GraphRAG.

Metrics: recall@k, nDCG, MRR, calibration of slates, diversity, and “did we amplify harm?”

Metric trap: clicks are not unbiased labels

Users can only click what was shown, and they click top positions regardless of relevance (position and exposure bias). Offline nDCG can rise while retention falls. Use inverse-propensity weighting, bandit exploration that gathers data on lower ranks, and online interleaving A/B tests — then optimise satisfaction/retention proxies alongside raw CTR.

Short notes

Recommenders predict preference; search predicts relevance. Both are ranking. Knowledge graphs give a symbolic spine for RAG so the generator is not the only memory.

Point notes

  • BM25 still wins more often than slides admit.
  • Feedback loops: popular items get more popular.
  • nDCG for ranking quality.

P1. Why can a recommender that maximises clicks harm users?

Clicks ≠ long-term value. Outrage and addiction click well. Feedback loops narrow the slate. Need delayed metrics, diversity constraints, and human values in the objective — not raw CTR.

Self-mark:

P2. Design a recommender for a grocery app that launched this week and has almost no interaction data.

Start content/popularity-based: category and cuisine tags, basket co-occurrence rules, regional best-sellers and an onboarding taste quiz; explicitly explore to collect interactions. As logs grow, add matrix factorisation/two-tower embeddings with a retrieve-then-rerank stack, mixing collaborative and content scores to keep new items covered (cold-start). Always keep a trending/editorial fallback and measure satisfaction/retention, not only clicks.

Self-mark:

P3. Why is industrial retrieval split into a cheap retrieve stage and an expensive rerank stage? Give the latency intuition.

Millions of items cannot each be scored by a heavy cross-encoder against the query in tens of milliseconds. Stage 1 uses cheap BM25 and/or ANN dense-vector search to pull a few hundred candidates (sub-linear index lookup, ~ms); stage 2 applies an expensive cross-encoder or gradient-boosted ranker with full query–item interaction to that small candidate set for precision. The same retrieve–rerank pattern powers RAG systems.

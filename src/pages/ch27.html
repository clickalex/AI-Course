Chapter 27 · PhD

Learning theory

When can you prove a model will generalise — and why deep nets ignore the old proofs and still work?

Figure 27.1 — Theory is the map of what cannot work, and the surprise of what does.

PAC learning: with high probability, empirical risk close to true risk, given enough samples vs hypothesis class complexity. VC dimension measures how many points a class can shatter. Uniform convergence + concentration inequalities (Hoeffding) are the machinery.

Deep nets have huge VC-dimension yet generalise. Research: implicit bias of SGD, double descent, grokking, interpolation, NTK, scaling laws (loss vs compute). Nonconvex optimisation: we don’t get global minima guarantees; we get empirics + some landscape results.

Information theory: cross-entropy is KL to the data distribution; bits-back, rate-distortion for compression/generative models.

Short notes

Classical bounds: complexity ↓ or n ↑ ⇒ generalisation. Deep learning broke the “must underfit to generalise” slogan (double descent). PhD work is: explain that, or exploit it (scaling, distillation, implicit regularisation).

Point notes

  • PAC: probably approximately correct.
  • VC: capacity of a hypothesis class.
  • Double descent: overparameterised models can generalise.
  • Scaling laws: predictable loss vs compute.

P1. Why don’t huge VC bounds “prove” that GPT-class models cannot generalise?

The bounds are worst-case over all hypotheses in a giant class. SGD + data structure + implicit bias pick a tiny subset. Vacuous bounds ≠ no generalisation. Research seeks tighter, algorithm-dependent bounds.

Self-mark:

P2. Give a high-bias and a high-variance model and explain how ensembles attack each error type.

High bias: a rigid linear model on a genuinely nonlinear problem underfits and errs systematically. High variance: an unregularised deep decision tree (or over-parameterised net on tiny data) swings wildly across training samples. Bagging (random forests) averages low-bias, high-variance learners trained on bootstrap samples to reduce variance; boosting sequentially fits residuals to drive bias down while shrinkage/depth control variance; more data and regularisation help both.

Self-mark:

P3. A 100M-parameter net reaches zero training error yet only 12% test error on 5,000 examples. Classical theory predicts disaster; give two reasons it may generalise and two checks to run.

Reasons: SGD's implicit bias plus architectural inductive biases (convolutions, attention) select simple interpolating solutions, and over-parameterised models exhibit double descent — test error can fall again past the interpolation threshold; the task may also be easy/redundant. Checks: leakage-free held-out (and grouped/cross-subject) validation with multiple seeds and confidence intervals, learning curves versus data size and parameter count, strong simple baselines for comparison, and audit of augmentation/preprocessing contamination. Zero train error is not itself evidence against generalisation.

School rules

  1. You must still understand the answer. Copying a bot essay is cheating and often false.
  2. Never paste passwords, Aadhaar, or private photos into a public bot.
  3. Check people-facts in a trusted source.
  4. Don’t generate mean images of classmates.

Short notes

AI maps inputs to outputs using data and/or rules. Useful and fallible. You stay responsible. Next: Chapter 1 uses grown-up words for the same ideas.

Point notes

  • Input → process → output.
  • Examples teach many modern AIs.
  • Fluent ≠ true.
  • Keep secrets off public chats.

MCQs

5 questions · scored

1. A chatbot writes a perfect-looking history paragraph. You should:

Hallucinations look confident. Understanding is the assignment.

2. Face unlock is mainly:

Task is specialised. Size doesn’t matter.

3. Do not paste into a public AI chat:

Treat chats as possibly stored. Secrets stay offline.

4. How does a voice assistant usually act when you speak to it?

Speech becomes features, a learned model predicts intent, and a tool answers — with no feelings involved.

5. A homework chatbot confidently gives a historical date that disagrees with your textbook. The best move is to:

Fluency is not factuality. Verify claims in trusted sources and cite them.

Practice

P1. Name three AI systems you used this week and one way each could be wrong.

Maps (wrong traffic), autocorrect (wrong word), video feed (wastes time), chatbot (fake citation), camera (misses a face). Point: useful + fallible.

Self-mark:

P2. A classmate says, “If the AI says it, it must be true.” How would you answer, with an example?

Fluent writing is not the same as truth. AI predicts patterns from its training data and can confidently invent facts (a fake citation, a wrong date, a made-up law). Example: a chatbot may list a book the author never wrote. Check people-facts in a trusted source, and you stay responsible for what you submit.

Self-mark:

P3. Draw input → process → output for a video app that suggests the next clip. What are the “examples” it learns from?

Inputs = watch history, clicks, skips, likes and time watched; process = a learned ranking model; output = the suggested list. The examples are millions of past interactions from many users (and your past sessions). Your clicks become new training data — which is why the feed can narrow toward what keeps you watching.
